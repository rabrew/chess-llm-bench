Abstract

Large language models (LLMs) write fluent explanations of almost anything, including chess. Does that fluency come from reasoning about the position, or from reproducing patterns in their training text? This project tests 22 open-source LLMs, from 2 to 70 billion parameters, on 4,000 Lichess puzzle positions. Each model is asked to evaluate the position, choose a move and explain who stands better, giving 526,662 scored answers. A neural chess engine, Leela Chess Zero, provides the correct answers. Every score is set against a chance baseline.

The models rarely produce a legal move unaided (9.3% of first answers). When given a list of legal moves, they pick the engine's best move barely more often than a random pick (10.2% against 8.8%). They play the human move that led into the puzzle, usually a mistake, more often than the best move. Their evaluations are no better than answering "equal" every time, and their explanations name the better side less often than a fixed guess. Moves are either essentially perfect (19%) or disastrous (42% blunders), with little in between. Larger models do no better, except in Google's Gemma families. Chain-of-thought prompting changes nothing. Fine-tuning five small models to read the board raised board-reading accuracy from 1–19% to 89–99%, but did not improve play, and legality fell. A check with ten times more engine search left the model ranking unchanged.

Taken together, the results are more consistent with pattern matching than with reasoning about the position. An audit of the project's own metrics found that naive measures made weak models look capable at every step. The audit itself is a result.

1. Introduction

Since 2022, large language models have gone from research curiosities to tools used by millions of people. They answer questions, write code and explain their answers in confident, well-formed prose. That fluency invites a question with real consequences: when a model explains why something is true, is it working the problem out, or reproducing text that looks like an answer?

The question is hard to settle in everyday tasks, because there is rarely a single right answer and a fluent answer is easy to mistake for a correct one. Chess avoids both problems. Its rules are fixed and completely specified. Every position has a correct evaluation and best move, which a chess engine can compute far more accurately than any human. Every claim a model makes about a position can be checked. Chess was proposed as a test bed for machine intelligence as early as 1950, for exactly this reason: it is "sharply defined both in allowed operations (the moves) and in the ultimate goal (mate)"[1] CHECK quote wording against Shannon's paper.

Chess is also a good test because language models were never designed to play it. A general-purpose LLM has seen chess only as text: game records, puzzle collections, forum posts and commentary. If it can still evaluate a new position, choose a good move and give a correct reason, that is evidence of something like reasoning. If it produces the form of a chess answer without its substance, that points to pattern matching.

This project measures three abilities separately, on the same positions:

  • T1, evaluation: how good is the position, in centipawns?
  • T2, move choice: what is the best move?
  • T3, explanation: who stands better, and why?

It then asks whether the results change with model size, prompt style or fine-tuning. Throughout, every score is compared with what a strategy with no chess knowledge would get. Without that comparison, models look far more capable than they are.

2. Chess and its notation

2.1 Positions as text: FEN

A language model receives only text, so the chess position has to be written down. The standard way is Forsyth–Edwards Notation (FEN). It is based on a nineteenth-century notation by David Forsyth, and Steven Edwards adapted it for computers as part of the Portable Game Notation (PGN) standard of 1994[2]. A FEN string describes a whole position on one line, in six fields:

  1. piece placement, rank by rank from rank 8 down to rank 1, with upper case for White, lower case for Black and digits for runs of empty squares
  2. side to move (w or b)
  3. castling rights
  4. en passant target square
  5. half-move clock (for the fifty-move rule)
  6. full-move number

rnbqkbnr/pppppppp/8/8/4P3/8/PPPP1PPP/RNBQKBNR b KQkq e3 0 1

The position after 1.e4. The pawn now on e4 appears as 4P3 on the fourth rank (four empty squares, a white pawn, three empty), and Black is to move.

FEN is compact and unambiguous, but it isn't easy for a person to read: they have to rebuild the board in their head. Whether it is also hard for a language model is a fair question, and section 12.2 addresses it directly.

2.2 Moves as text: SAN

Moves are written in Standard Algebraic Notation (SAN), also defined in the PGN standard[2]. A move names the piece (K, Q, R, B, N; nothing for a pawn) and its destination square: Nf3, e4. An x marks a capture (Bxc6), + check, # mate, O-O and O-O-O castling, and e8=Q promotion. A move is legal only if the rules allow it in that exact position for the side to move. A move that would leave your own king in check is illegal, for example, and a pinned piece cannot leave the line of the pin.

2.3 Evaluation, tactics and puzzles

Chess players and engines measure advantage in centipawns (cp): hundredths of a pawn. +150 means White is about one and a half pawns better; −300 means Black is about a minor piece better. By convention, evaluations are given from White's point of view.

A tactic is a short, forcing sequence that wins material or gives mate, such as a fork, a pin, a skewer or a mating attack. The Lichess puzzle database is a free, public collection of over six million positions from real games on lichess.org, each rated for difficulty and tagged with tactical themes[3]. Each puzzle starts one move before the tactic: the opponent's move (here called the setup move, usually a mistake) creates the opportunity, and the solver must find the reply. This detail matters for interpreting the results (section 13).

3. How chess engines work

3.1 Search: minimax and alpha-beta

Shannon's 1950 paper laid out the approach classical chess programs still follow[1]: look ahead through the tree of possible moves and replies, score the positions at the end with an evaluation function, and pass the scores back up the tree. Minimax assumes each side picks the move best for itself. The tree grows explosively, since a typical position has 30 or so legal moves, so engines prune it. Alpha-beta pruning skips branches that provably cannot change the final choice. With good move ordering, an engine can search much deeper for the same work[4].

3.2 Evaluation: hand-written, then learned

For decades, evaluation functions were written by hand from chess knowledge: material, king safety, piece activity, pawn structure. In 2020, Stockfish, the strongest classical engine, adopted an "efficiently updatable neural network" (NNUE). NNUE is a small network, first developed for the Japanese game shogi, that can be updated cheaply as pieces move[5]. Stockfish still relies on deep alpha-beta search; only its evaluation became learned.

3.3 AlphaZero and Monte Carlo tree search

DeepMind's AlphaZero (2018) took a different route[6]. It learned chess, shogi and Go from self-play alone, given only the rules. A deep neural network looks at a position and outputs two things:

  • a value: how likely the side to move is to win
  • a policy: which moves look promising

Instead of alpha-beta, AlphaZero uses Monte Carlo tree search (MCTS). The search repeatedly picks a line to explore, guided by the policy and by the values found so far, and spends more effort on promising moves[6][7]. Each position the search visits is called a node. More nodes means a deeper, more reliable search.

3.4 Leela Chess Zero (Lc0): this project's ground truth

Leela Chess Zero (Lc0) is an open-source engine that follows the AlphaZero approach. It was announced in January 2018, is developed by volunteers, and is released under the GPLv3 licence[8]. Lc0 runs on a graphics card (GPU). That made it practical here: it had to check more than half a million answers on one home PC. The project uses it in two ways:

  • T1 evaluation truth: a single pass of the network, with no search, converted to centipawns and capped at ±10,000.
  • T2 move truth: a search of 800 nodes per position, giving the best move and the evaluations used to score move quality.

Is 800 nodes enough? A stronger search might judge some moves differently. Section 11.8 tests this directly: re-scoring 1,000 positions at 8,000 nodes left the ranking of models unchanged.

3.5 Measuring move quality

Centipawn loss (CPL) measures how much worse a position is after the model's move than after the engine's best move, from the mover's point of view. A CPL of 0 means the best move. A CPL of 300 means the move threw away about a minor piece. Raw CPL has a problem: a move that misses a forced mate can score a loss of thousands of centipawns, and a few such moves swamp the average (an early version of this project reported a mean CPL of 4,735). Following common practice, both evaluations are clamped to ±1,000 cp before taking the difference CHECK cite the Lichess source for the ±1,000 clamp. A blunder is defined here as a clamped CPL of 1,000 or more: a move that turns a winning position into a lost one, or worse.

4. How language models work

4.1 Tokens and next-token prediction

A language model reads and writes tokens: short chunks of text, often a word or part of a word. A FEN string such as r1bqkbnr/pppp1ppp/2n5/… is split into tokens too. The model has no built-in idea that these characters describe an 8×8 board.

A model is trained by showing it huge amounts of text and adjusting it to predict the next token better. Everything it "knows" comes from statistical patterns in that text. Chess is part of the web, so the training text includes game records, puzzles, FEN strings and annotated analysis. One influential paper argues that LLMs are best understood through this training objective. They do much better when the task, the input and the correct output are all likely under the training data, and worse when they are not[9].

4.2 Transformers and attention

Modern LLMs are built on the Transformer architecture[10]. Its key mechanism, attention, lets each token draw on information from every earlier token, weighted by how relevant it is. A model can only attend to a limited number of tokens at once, its context window. If a prompt is longer than the window, the excess is cut off. This mattered in practice for one model in this project (section 13).

4.3 From base models to assistants and "reasoning" models

A model trained only on next-token prediction is a base model. The models tested here are instruction-tuned: trained further to follow instructions and answer in a chat format. Some newer models are trained to write out intermediate steps before answering. DeepSeek-R1, two of whose distilled versions are tested here, was trained with reinforcement learning to produce long reasoning chains[11]. Simply asking a model to "think step by step" is called chain-of-thought prompting. In very large models, it improves results on arithmetic and logic problems[12]. This project tests whether it helps with chess.

4.4 Size, quantisation and running models locally

Model size is counted in parameters, the numbers adjusted during training. The models here range from 2 to 70 billion. Larger models trained on more data reliably reach lower prediction error, following smooth "scaling laws"[13]. A natural guess is that bigger models will also reason better, which this project tests (H4). To run large models on a home PC, their parameters are quantised: stored in about 4 bits instead of 16, which cuts memory roughly fourfold. All models were run through Ollama, which runs quantised models locally CHECK cite Ollama (ollama.com) and the GGUF/llama.cpp format.

4.5 Sampling and temperature

A model doesn't produce one fixed answer. At each step it gives a probability for every possible next token, and a token is drawn from that distribution. The temperature setting controls how much randomness the draw allows. All models here ran at Ollama's default temperature, so the same prompt can give different answers. The project measured how much (section 11.7).

5. Do language models reason?

Whether LLMs reason or "merely" match patterns is one of the central debates about them. Two positions mark out the ends of it.

The sceptical view. Bender and Koller argued that a system trained only on linguistic form cannot learn meaning[14]. Bender, Gebru and colleagues described LLMs as "stochastic parrots" that stitch together sequences of words probabilistically, without reference to meaning[15]. Recent experiments support a version of this view:

  • GSM-Symbolic: Apple researchers took grade-school maths problems and changed only the numbers or added one irrelevant sentence. Model accuracy dropped, in the worst cases sharply. The authors suggest the models replicate reasoning steps seen in training rather than reasoning formally[16].
  • Faith and Fate: Dziri and colleagues found that Transformers solve multi-step problems such as multiplication and logic puzzles largely by matching sub-patterns seen in training. Accuracy falls quickly as problems grow beyond the training distribution[17].

The "world model" view. On the other side, some studies find structured internal representations. Li and colleagues trained a GPT-style model only on lists of legal Othello moves. They found that it built an internal representation of the board, and that editing that representation changed its predictions[18]. Karvonen extended this to chess: a small model trained only on chess game records developed internal board representations, and even an estimate of the players' skill[19].

Where this project fits. Those world-model studies trained small, specialised models on games alone. The models tested here are general-purpose chat models, for which chess is a tiny fraction of training. The question here is not whether a model could learn a board representation, but whether widely used models do reason about positions when asked. The answer is judged by what they get right, compared against chance.

Contamination. Benchmarks are only meaningful if the test items weren't in the training data. Sainz and colleagues argue this "data contamination" is widespread, hard to measure, and can lead to wrong conclusions[20]. Lichess puzzles are public, so the models may have seen these positions. Section 13 explains why that makes this project's negative results stronger, not weaker.

6. Language models and chess: previous work

  • LLMs can sometimes play. In 2023, Nicholas Carlini reported that OpenAI's GPT-3.5-turbo-instruct, given a game as a PGN move list, could play reasonable chess and beat him, while GPT-4 made illegal moves[21]. Widely shared estimates put its strength around 1,800 Elo, but that figure is disputed and not from a controlled study CHECK whether Carlini's post gives its own Elo estimate before quoting one. Format mattered: the same family of models did much worse in chat form.
  • Transformers trained for chess can be very strong. DeepMind trained a 270-million-parameter Transformer to predict a strong engine's judgements on positions from ten million games. It reached a Lichess blitz rating of about 2,895 against humans without any search[22]. The architecture isn't the limit. A model built and trained for chess can be very strong.
  • Models trained on games build board representations[19], as above.

The gap this project fills. Most previous work tests one model, one task (usually playing games), or a model trained specifically for chess. This project tests many general-purpose open models (22, from 2B to 70B) on 4,000 real positions across difficulty levels. It uses three separate tasks (evaluate, move, explain) with engine-checked answers, compares every score with a chance baseline, and tests whether fine-tuning on board reading helps. 7. Fine-tuning

Fine-tuning means continuing to train an existing model on a small, focused dataset so it learns a specific skill. Updating every parameter of a 7B model needs far more GPU memory than a home PC has. LoRA (low-rank adaptation) freezes the original weights and trains small add-on matrices in each layer. That cuts the number of trainable parameters by orders of magnitude, with little loss in quality[23]. QLoRA goes further by keeping the frozen model in 4-bit form while training the adapters[24]. This project used 4-bit QLoRA (rank 16, alpha 32) to fine-tune five 3–7B models on one RTX 5080 graphics card. CHECK confirm rank/alpha/epochs for the pieces-only run in fine-tuning/config/finetune_config.yaml

Why fine-tune at all? If models fail at chess because they can't picture the board from a FEN, teaching them to read it should help. That makes it a direct test of the "they just can't read the board" explanation. Two versions were trained: one that lists every piece and its square, and one that also lists every legal move.

Held-out data. A model tested on positions it trained on measures memory, not skill. None of the 20,000 training positions were among the 4,000 benchmark positions. A further 200 positions were held out from training to check that each model had learned its task.

8. Measuring fairly

8.1 Baselines

A score means little on its own. "Picks the best move 10% of the time" sounds poor. But if a random legal move would also be best 9% of the time, it is barely better than chance. Every measure here is compared with a simple strategy that uses no chess knowledge:

  • Move choice: a uniformly random legal move. Positions here have about 27 legal moves on average, but the chance level is the average of 1 ÷ (number of legal moves) over the actual positions (8.8%), not 1 ÷ 27.
  • Evaluation: answering "0" (equal) every time.
  • Who is better: always naming whichever side is better most often in the dataset.

8.2 Rank correlation

Spearman's rank correlation (ρ) measures whether two sets of numbers go up and down together, using only their order[25]. ρ = 1 means the model ranks positions in exactly the same order as the engine. ρ = 0 means no relationship. Using ranks means a few huge evaluations (such as forced mates) can't dominate the result.

8.3 Agreement beyond chance: Cohen's κ

If 54% of positions favour one side, a model that always names that side is "right" 54% of the time without looking at the board. Cohen's kappa (κ) corrects agreement for what the label frequencies alone would produce[26]. κ = 0 means no better than chance, κ = 1 means perfect agreement.

8.4 Confidence intervals and the bootstrap

Any rate measured on a sample would come out slightly differently on another sample. The bootstrap estimates that uncertainty by resampling the data with replacement many times and recomputing the statistic each time[27]. The middle 95% of those values gives a 95% confidence interval. Two models whose intervals overlap are treated as statistically tied. All resampling here uses a fixed seed (42), so the intervals reproduce exactly.

8.5 Deciding the pass mark in advance

If you choose what counts as success after seeing the results, it's easy, even unintentionally, to pick the version that looks best. To avoid this, the pass marks for the legal-moves fine-tune, the engine-strength check and the plain-board experiment were written down and committed to the project's version history before any results existed. This practice is called pre-registration CHECK add a general source on pre-registration, e.g. Nosek et al. 2018, PNAS. The pieces-only fine-tune's criteria came after its results, and the write-up says so.

9. Research questions and hypotheses

Main question: do open-source LLMs reason about chess positions, or do they pattern-match?

  • H1–H3 (difficulty): evaluation error (H1), move quality (H2) and explanation quality (H3) get worse as puzzle difficulty rises.
  • H4 (size): within a model family, larger models do better.
  • Prompting: does asking for step-by-step reasoning help?
  • Fine-tuning: does teaching a model to read the board, and then the legal moves, improve its evaluations, moves and explanations?
  • Notation (in progress): do the models do better if the position is given as a plain board diagram instead of a FEN?

If the models reason, we'd expect performance well above chance, gains with size, and gains when they're helped to read the board. If they pattern-match, we'd expect near-chance performance, familiar-looking but wrong answers, and little benefit from extra help.

10. Method

10.1 Positions

Positions come from the Lichess puzzle database[3]. They were grouped into four tiers by puzzle rating: easy (below 1,200), medium (1,200–1,800), hard (1,800–2,400) and extreme (2,400 and above). Then 1,000 per tier were drawn with a fixed random seed (42), giving 4,000 positions. Each position is the one before the puzzle's setup move: the position the human was in when they made it.

10.2 Models

Twenty-two open-source models from ten families were run locally through Ollama: Llama 3 (3B, 8B, 70B), Qwen 2.5 (7B, 14B, 32B), Gemma 3 (4B, 12B), Gemma 4 (E2B, E4B, 26B, 31B), DeepSeek-R1 distilled (7B, 14B), Mistral 7B, Mixtral 8×7B, Phi-4 14B, Solar 10.7B, Yi 34B, Command-R 35B, CodeLlama 34B and WizardLM-2 7B. CHECK cite each model's release page or paper in an appendix table

10.3 Prompts and tasks

Each position was given to each model in six prompt formats:

  • Three combined prompts asking all three questions at once:
    • fen_only: the FEN alone
    • pgn+fen: the FEN plus the moves played so far
    • cot: the FEN plus an instruction to think step by step
  • Three single-task prompts (eval_only, move_only, explanation_only), so each ability could be measured without the others getting in the way.

The combined prompt asked for a reply in a fixed format:

Eval: <integer>
Move: <SAN move>
Explanation: <White / Black / Equal> — <one sentence reason>

That gives 4,000 positions × 22 models × 6 formats ≈ 528,000 jobs. After failures, 526,662 answers were scored. In practice pgn+fen turned out identical to fen_only, because none of the positions had a stored move history (Issue 23).

10.4 Getting a legal move

Most first answers were illegal, so on combined prompts a move was accepted in three steps:

  1. take the model's Move: answer
  2. if it was illegal, look for any legal move elsewhere in the reply
  3. if that failed, ask again with a list of every legal move and an instruction to pick one

The "final" legality figure therefore includes copying from a list. The raw replies were saved in the later runs, so the model's own first answer can be scored separately. This distinction is one of the most important findings (section 11.2).

10.5 Scoring

  • T1: Spearman ρ between the model's evaluation and Lc0's, plus the model's average error divided by the error of always answering 0.
  • T2: legality; clamped CPL against Lc0 at 800 nodes; blunder rate (clamped CPL ≥ 1,000) with bootstrap 95% CIs; best-move rate against a random legal move; and how often the model played the human setup move.
  • T3: one mark for naming the better side (judged at ±50 cp, with κ against the majority baseline), and one mark for naming a theme of the puzzle, using whole-word matching. The 0–2 T3 score is the sum.

10.6 Fine-tuning

Five models (Llama 3.2 3B, Gemma 3 4B, Qwen 2.5 7B, Mistral 7B, DeepSeek-R1 7B) were fine-tuned with QLoRA on 20,000 positions, none from the benchmark, in two versions:

  • pieces only: list every piece and its square from a FEN
  • pieces + legal moves: the same, plus every legal move in sorted SAN

Each fine-tuned model was run on the same benchmark and compared with its base model re-run on the same code, so any difference comes from the training alone.

10.7 The audit

Before writing up, every metric was audited against the raw data. Each defect found got a written specification, automated tests and an entry in the project's issues log (Issues 14–25). Every table in the results is generated from the data by a script, and a check fails if any table no longer matches. The audit is summarised in section 11.10.

10.8 Reproducibility

The positions (seed 42), the engine truth and all scoring are fully reproducible from the code and data. Each results summary records the exact code version (git commit), a fingerprint of the data, and the engine settings. The model outputs are not exactly reproducible, because the models sample at their default temperature (section 11.7).

The distribution has two peaks and almost nothing in between. A player who understood the position would make many "decent" moves and few disasters. These models either find a good move or throw the position away, more often the latter.

Compared with chance, the picture is starker. Models played the engine's best move 10.2% of the time. A random legal move would have been best 8.8% of the time on the same positions, so the models are only 1.16× better than random. Most models sit between 0.94× and 1.23×; only Gemma 4 31B and 26B reach about 1.5–1.6×. Pooled, the models played the human's setup move, usually the mistake that allowed the tactic, 11.5% of the time. That's more often than they found the engine's best move. Reproducing a familiar human move instead of calculating is what pattern matching would predict.

The best models by blunder rate were Gemma 4 31B (35.8%) and 26B (37.3%), clearly separated from the rest (40–45%). Neighbouring models elsewhere in the ranking have overlapping confidence intervals and should be treated as tied.

The 97% legality figure is almost entirely the effect of the re-ask with a list. Left to themselves, models produce a legal move about one time in ten. The illegal answers are revealing: they are mostly stock opening moves that ignore the position. Llama 3.2 3B answered "e4" 45% of the time, Qwen 2.5 7B "Nf6" 24%, and Gemma 3 4B "d5" 20%, often for the wrong side or onto an occupied square. These look like the most common chess moves in text, not answers to the position on the board.

11.3 Evaluations don't follow the position

The rank correlation between model and engine evaluations is 0.11 pooled, and at most 0.25 for any model. No model's average error beats answering "0" every time: the ratio runs from 1.31 to 7.03. Most positions are near level, so a model that always guesses a small number gets a low error without reading the board. That is why raw error alone made weak models look good (Issue 14).

"Who is winning" accuracy rises as the threshold widens (44.5% at ±0 cp, 61.6% at ±100, 78.9% at ±200). Cohen's κ stays at 0.02–0.07 at every threshold, so the high raw figures come from the class balance, not from reading the board. Splitting by side to move showed that no model reports evaluations from the wrong side's point of view.

11.4 Explanations name the better side less often than a fixed guess

Models named the better side correctly 30.3% of the time. Always naming the side that is usually better would score 54.2%, and κ is 0.07. Models rarely say "Equal", so they lose the mark on level positions almost automatically. They named a tactical theme of the puzzle in 6.0% of explanations. That mark is reported but not used as evidence, because the tactic usually isn't on the board yet in the position shown (section 13).

11.5 Size does not predict performance, except within Gemma

  • Llama 3: blunder rate 41.1% (3B) → 44.4% (8B) → 43.2% (70B). No gain from 23× the parameters.
  • Qwen 2.5: 42.1% (7B) → 42.3% (14B) → 44.2% (32B), slightly worse.
  • DeepSeek-R1: 7B → 14B, no improvement.
  • Gemma 4: blunder rate 44.5% → 42.6% → 37.3% → 35.8% and ρ 0.11 → 0.24, from 2B to 31B.
  • Gemma 3: 4B → 12B improves on all three measures (blunder rate 43.0% → 41.0%, ρ 0.03 → 0.20).

H4 holds for both Gemma families and fails for Llama 3, Qwen 2.5 and DeepSeek-R1, so it is not supported as a general rule. That one developer's models improve with size while others don't points to differences in training data or method, not size itself.

11.6 Difficulty (H1–H3)

None of H1–H3 is supported: scores are flat across tiers. But the tiers are Lichess puzzle ratings, which describe the position after the setup move, not the one the models saw. So the flat result says nothing reliable about difficulty, and no conclusion rests on it.

Asking for step-by-step reasoning gave exactly the same blunder rate as the plain prompt. Chain-of-thought gains have been reported mainly for very large models on maths and logic problems[12]; they don't appear here.

Because pgn+fen repeated fen_only, it also served as a repeatability test. Given the identical prompt twice, the same model gave the same move only 68.7% of the time, named the same better side 75.0% of the time, and gave an identical evaluation 34.2% of the time.

11.8 The engine check: does a stronger search change anything?

1,000 positions (85,102 moves) were re-scored with Lc0 at 8,000 nodes, ten times the original search. The pass mark was set in advance: the ranking holds if Spearman ρ between the models' blunder rates at the two strengths is at least 0.9. It was 0.97. Re-running at 800 nodes gives 0.99, which shows how much the engine varies on its own. No model moved more than four places, and the overall blunder rate went from 42.0% to 42.1%. The conclusions don't depend on the engine's search depth.

Pieces + legal moves. This version's criteria were fixed before training. The first criterion asked whether the training itself worked: legal-move recall and precision of at least 0.9 on held-out positions. It failed for all five models: they listed only 65–81% of the legal moves, and only 52–60% of what they listed was legal. By the rule fixed in advance, the training didn't take, so no conclusion about transfer can be drawn. What it does show: even approximate knowledge of the legal moves made answers less legal, not more (final legality fell by 6 to 69 points).

12. Discussion

12.1 Pattern matching, not reasoning

Five independent lines of evidence point the same way:

  1. The models rarely produce a legal move unaided.
  2. Their move choice is barely above chance, and they prefer the familiar human move to the best one.
  3. Move quality is all-or-nothing.
  4. Their evaluations don't track the position.
  5. Size doesn't help, outside one developer's models.

None of these proves an absence of reasoning on its own, and "pattern matching" is a description, not a mechanism. But each is what pattern matching predicts, and each is hard to explain if the models were reasoning about the position. The illegal first answers are especially telling. "e4", "Nf6" and "d5" are among the most common moves in chess text. A system predicting likely text would produce them. A system reasoning about the position in front of it would not. This matches McCoy and colleagues' account: models do well when the expected output is common in their training text and poorly otherwise[9]. It also matches the GSM-Symbolic and Faith-and-Fate findings in other domains[16][17].

The world-model studies[18][19] aren't contradicted. They show that a model trained only on games can build a board representation. These results suggest that general chat models don't use one when asked about chess, or don't have a reliable one, at least at the sizes tested. DeepMind's searchless chess model[22] shows the Transformer architecture isn't the limit. What's missing in general chat models is the right training.

12.2 Is this just a notation test?

The most obvious objection is that FEN is hard to read, so the models may be failing at notation rather than chess. The project has four answers to this, plus a direct test.

  1. Fixing the reading didn't help. The pieces-only fine-tune took board reading from 1–19% to 89–99%. If notation were the bottleneck, evaluations and moves should have improved. They didn't, and legality got worse.
  2. Choosing from a list needs no notation skill, and is still near chance. On the re-ask, the models pick from a list of legal moves. They still choose the best move only 10.2% of the time, against 8.8% for a random pick.
  3. Answers with no notation are still below a fixed guess. "Which side is better" is answered with one word, yet models score 30.3% against a 54.2% baseline.
  4. FEN is a fair format for a language model. It's common on chess websites and forums, so it's very likely in the training data CHECK find a source or rephrase as reasoning. It's also the standard input in chess-LLM research. Hard for a person to read isn't the same as hard for a text model.
  5. The direct test. The plain-board experiment gives 21 models the same positions as a board diagram and a plain-English piece list, with no FEN anywhere, against a FEN control in the same run. PLACEHOLDER: result and verdict

12.3 Why do the Gemma models scale?

The Gemma families are the one place where bigger is clearly better. This project can't say why. Differences in training data (more chess or more structured reasoning data), training method, or tokenisation are all possible. It's a good question for future work, and a reminder that "LLMs" aren't one thing.

12.4 Why did fine-tuning make legality worse?

A plausible explanation, not tested here, is that the fine-tuning shifted the models' outputs towards producing board-like text, such as square names and piece lists, at the expense of following the move-answer format. The failure analysis supports part of this: failed answers were well-formed moves that were illegal, often for the wrong side (1,080 of 3,024 for Gemma 3 4B). Either way, the result supports the main conclusion. Supplying knowledge that a reasoning system could use did not produce better reasoning.

12.5 What the audit teaches

The corrections in section 11.10 aren't just housekeeping. Each naive metric was a reasonable first choice, and each one overstated the models. This fits the wider concern that benchmark numbers for LLMs can mislead without careful controls[20]. The practical lessons are: always report a chance baseline, separate what the model did from what the scaffolding did, and fix pass marks in advance.

13. Limitations

  • Scope. Open-source models from 2B to 70B, run locally, on chess only. No claims are made about commercial models such as GPT-4 or Claude, or about reasoning in general.
  • Tactical positions. Puzzles are tactical by construction. Quiet, strategic positions may behave differently.
  • Contamination. Lichess puzzles are public and may be in the training data[20]. If so, the models did badly even on positions they may have seen, which makes the result more striking, not less.
  • Setup position (Issue 21). The benchmark shows the position before the setup move. T1, T2 and the side mark are valid on that position. The theme mark and the difficulty tiers describe the next position, so they're reported but not used as evidence.
  • Move history not tested. No position had a stored game history, so pgn+fen repeated fen_only.
  • Reproducibility. Model outputs vary between runs (68.7% same-move agreement).
  • First-answer legality is only available for five models. The plain-board run adds it for all 21 tested there.
  • Llama 3.3 70B context. Its 512-token context window was shorter than 8.7% of move_only prompts, which were therefore cut off. This affects its move_only and re-asked moves.
  • DeepSeek-R1 thinking mode was probably on in the April runs and off in the October re-run. The results match within 0.3 points.
  • T3 is marked by rules (side named, theme words present). It checks whether the right words appear, not whether the reasoning is sound.
  • Fine-tuning scope. Only five 3–7B models were fine-tuned. Training larger models needs more GPU memory than a home PC has. The legal-moves version did not reach its own training bar.
  • Missing data. DeepSeek-R1 7B is missing about 4% of its base-run answers.

14. Conclusion and future work

Across 22 open-source models, 4,000 positions and more than half a million answers, the models evaluated, chose moves and explained positions at or near the level of strategies that use no chess knowledge at all. Their typical errors were the most common moves in chess text, played regardless of the position. Bigger models, step-by-step prompting and teaching them to read the board did not change this, except for the larger Gemma models. A stronger engine left the conclusions unchanged. The evidence is consistent with general-purpose LLMs producing the form of chess analysis without reasoning about the position. A second, practical conclusion is that naive benchmark metrics can make this look otherwise. Every figure needs a baseline, and the system around the model must be kept separate from the model itself.

Future work:

  • Re-run on the actual puzzle positions, so the theme and difficulty measures become valid evidence.
  • Test whether game history helps, using positions with real move histories.
  • Train the legal-moves version longer until it passes its own bar, then test transfer.
  • Fine-tune larger models on cloud hardware.
  • Investigate why the Gemma families scale when others don't.
  • Compare with commercial models.
