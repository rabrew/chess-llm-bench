Do LLMs reason? Benchmarking logical performance via chess

Language models write about chess fluently. This project tests whether they can actually work out a position, or whether they produce answers that only look like chess. 22 open-source models, 4,000 positions, 526,662 answers, all checked against a chess engine.

01 Hypothesis02 Experiment03 Results04 ConclusionLimitations

01Question and hypotheses

Research question: do open-source language models reason about a chess position, or do they pattern-match on familiar chess text?

Chess is a good test of reasoning. The rules are fixed, every answer can be checked exactly, and an engine stronger than any human gives the right answer. A model that reasons should find legal, sensible moves and judge who is winning. A model that pattern-matches should produce answers that look right but don't depend on the actual position.

Main hypothesis. If the models reason, their answers will track the position: legal moves, accuracy well above chance, and evaluations that agree with the engine. If they pattern-match, their answers will look like chess but sit close to chance and trivial baselines.

H5Models do relatively better at explaining than at moving in openings, and worse in endgames

02Experiment

Three tasks for every position

T1 · Evaluate

Give the position's score in centipawns. Compared with the engine by rank correlation (ρ), and with always answering 0.

T2 · Move

Choose the best move. Scored as legal or not, then by how much it loses compared with the engine's move (blunder = 1,000+ centipawns).

T3 · Explain

Say who stands better and why. Marked for naming the right side, against always naming the most common answer.

Materials

  • Positions: 4,000 Lichess puzzle positions, 1,000 in each of four rating tiers, drawn with a fixed seed (42).
  • Models: 22 open-source models from 2B to 70B parameters, in 12 families (Llama, Gemma, Qwen, DeepSeek-R1, Mistral and others), run locally through Ollama on one PC (RTX 5080).
  • Prompts: six formats: three ask all three questions together (plain, with move history, and step-by-step "chain of thought"), three ask one question each.
  • Ground truth: the Lc0 chess engine. One network pass for T1, an 800-node search for T2.
  • Fairness checks: every measure has a chance or trivial baseline, chance-corrected agreement (Cohen's κ), and bootstrap 95% confidence intervals.

Seven experiments

1 · Move choice is barely better than chance

Models played the engine's best move 10.2% of the time; a random legal move would manage 8.8%. They played the human move from the position, usually the mistake that set up the puzzle, more often (11.5%). Moves were "perfect or disaster": 18.7% essentially perfect, 41.9% blunders, few in between.

The largest Gemma 4 models have the lowest point estimates. Adjacent ranks are not automatically significant; the chart does not display model-level uncertainty.

2 · Without help, models rarely produce a legal move

Only 9.3% of first answers were legal. The illegal ones were mostly stock opening moves that ignore the board: llama3.2:3b answered "e4" 45% of the time.

3 · Evaluations don't follow the position

No model's evaluation error beat answering "0" every time (1.3× to 7× worse). The best rank correlation with the engine was 0.25. "Who is winning" agreement, corrected for chance, was κ 0.02–0.07.

4 · Explanations name the better side less often than a fixed guess

Models named the better side 30.3% of the time; always giving the most common answer scores 54.2% (κ = 0.07).

5 · Size doesn't help, except for Google's Gemma

Llama 3 from 3B to 70B: blunder rate 41.1% → 43.2%, no gain from 23× the parameters. Qwen 2.5 from 7B to 32B: slightly worse. Gemma 4 from 2B to 31B: 44.5% → 35.8%. When one developer's models scale and others' don't, the difference is more likely training data than size.

6 · Step-by-step prompting doesn't help

Chain-of-thought prompts gave the same blunder rate as the plain prompt (41.5% vs 41.5%). Asked the identical question twice, a model gave the same move only 68.7% of the time.

7 · Teaching the board didn't teach the game

Fine-tuning five small models (3–7B) to list the pieces raised board reading from 1–19% to 89–99%. Matched pooled final legality fell 95.88% → 76.89% and legal/scored non-blunder answers fell 56.21% → 43.96% of requests. Same-pair blunders changed 42.36% → 42.77%; some conditional measures improved. The legal-moves version didn't learn the rules well enough to pass its own pre-set check (65–81% of legal moves listed), so it can't answer whether knowing the rules would help.

8 · The engine isn't the reason

Re-scoring 85,102 moves with a 10× stronger engine kept the model ranking (ρ = 0.97), and the overall blunder rate barely moved (42.0% → 42.1%).

Claude: stronger but limited frontier competence

Haiku 5.5 and Opus 5.5 low-effort each answered 500 positions in three formats (1,500 records each). Their FEN first-answer legality was 88.8% and 93.8%; evaluation ρ was 0.4868 and 0.5769; side κ was 0.2621 and 0.3625. Assisted final FEN blunders were 29.0% and 19.2%, and engine-best moves 17.8% and 26.8%, versus 8.04% for random legal moves (2.21× and 3.33×).

C1, 498 identical legal/scored pairs: Gemma4:31b blunders were 34.5382%. Haiku scored 28.9157%: “no clear difference”, because the confidence intervals overlap despite a 5.6225-point reduction. Opus scored 19.2771%: “frontier model plays clearly better”, with a 15.2610-point reduction and non-overlapping intervals. These matched rates differ from the descriptive full-500 rates above.

C4: both receive the registered label “notation was a major barrier”. Its absolute plain-board ρ ≥ 0.30 branch triggers even though paired ρ falls from FEN for both variants. This formal result does not demonstrate a formatting gain. Sonnet C2/C3 are not run; pilot replies do not replace them.

Recorded Claude cost, including retries and Sonnet pilots, is $12.2555896 (Haiku $0.8918706; Opus $11.058668; Sonnet pilots $0.305051). Offline scoring adds no API charge. Parameter counts are unknown; Claude used the API’s default sampling settings because temperature could not be set. Public-puzzle contamination, assistance and output caps limit interpretation; Haiku had 41 capped initial replies.

The separate tool-disabled GPT illustration has only 12 positions (10 legal first moves, 12 legal final moves, six correct sides): illustrative only. The Sol/Luna 20-position-per-model API pilot tests setup and cost; capped replies prevent treating it as a completed frontier benchmark. No mini/nano experiment was run.

10 · Simple metrics overstate the models

Corrections changed the interpretation and uncertainty: legality 64.6% (miscounted) → 9.3% first answer; best move "3–4× chance" → 1.16×; evaluation "low error" → worse than answering 0. Fair baselines matter.

A caution on H1–H3: the difficulty tiers are Lichess ratings of the puzzle that follows the position the models saw, not of that position itself. A flat result therefore says little about difficulty, and no conclusion rests on it.

04Conclusion

The tested local models show weak chess competence; frontier Claude models show stronger but limited competence. Opus meets C1’s clear-better criterion; Haiku does not. The local notation verdict is mixed, and the fine-tunes did not reliably improve overall play. These behavioural results cannot identify an internal reasoning mechanism or establish a claim about every LLM.

Limitations

  • Scope: 22 local models (2B–70B) and two full Claude variants. Claude parameter counts are unknown; Sonnet C2/C3 were not run.
  • Difficulty tiers describe the next position, not the one shown, so H1–H3 can't be tested properly.
  • Explanations are marked by rules, checking for the right words, not judging the reasoning.
  • Model outputs aren't exactly repeatable (default temperature). The positions, engine values and scoring are.
  • Fine-tuning covered five small models only, and the legal-moves version didn't learn its target.
  • Contamination: the puzzles are public and may be in the models' training data, which limits claims about generalization, especially for frontier successes.
  • Claude settings: API defaults; temperature could not be set. Caps, assisted final moves and possible public-puzzle contamination remain limitations.
