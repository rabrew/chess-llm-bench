What the T1, T2 and T3 scores mean

This project tests whether AI chatbots actually understand chess, or just repeat patterns they have seen. Each chatbot was shown 4,000 chess positions and asked three questions about each one. Every question gets its own score. This page explains each score in plain English, and what the results showed.

Updated 29 October 2026, after the audit that corrected several earlier numbers.

A few words used on this page

Model
One of the 22 AI chatbots being tested. They are free ones that run on a home computer, from small (2 billion parameters) to very large (70 billion).
Engine
A chess program that plays far better than any human. This project uses one called Lc0. Its answers are treated as the correct answers.
Position
A snapshot of a chess board partway through a game. All 4,000 come from chess puzzles on the website Lichess.
Centipawn
The unit chess programs use to say who is ahead. 100 centipawns means one side is ahead by about one pawn. A plus number means White is ahead, a minus number means Black is ahead, and 0 means it's even.

T1 Who is winning, and by how much?

"Give me a number for this position."

The model gives a single number saying who is ahead and by how much. Its number is compared with the engine's.

How it's scored

  • Do its numbers move with the engine's? Across all positions, when the engine says one position is better for White than another, does the model agree? This is measured with a rank correlation: 1 means the model always gets the order right, 0 means there is no link at all.
  • Does it beat a lazy answer? Most positions are close to even, so answering "0" every time gets a fairly small error without looking at the board. A model's error is compared with the error of always saying 0.
Example

Over three positions the engine says +50, +300, −400. A model that says +20, +150, −100 is off by quite a lot, but it has the order right: it knows which position is best for White and which is worst. Rank correlation rewards that.

0.11rank correlation, all models together (0 = no link, 1 = perfect)
0.25the best any single model managed (Llama 3.3 70B)
3× worsethan always answering 0, on average. No model beat it.

The catch that changed the story: "picked the right side" was mostly luck

An earlier version counted how often the model picked the right side (White, Black or even). That looked good, up to 79%. But because most positions are close to even, a model answering "even" every time would score about as well. After correcting for that, the models agree with the engine barely more than chance.

T2 What is the best move?

"What move would you play here?"

The model names one move. That move is checked in two steps.

How it's scored

  • Is the move allowed? The computer checks it follows the rules of chess. For example, a bishop can't move in a straight line.
  • How much worse is it than the best move? The engine works out how good the position is after the model's move and compares it with the engine's own best move. The difference is the centipawn loss. 0 means just as good as the engine's move. 1000 or more is a blunder: giving away the queen, or walking into checkmate.
Example

Before the move, White is ahead by 150. After the model's move, Black is ahead by 200.

The move threw away 350 centipawns, so its centipawn loss is 350. Not a blunder, but a bad move.

Barely better than guessing

  • The models play the engine's best move 10.2% of the time. Picking a random legal move would get it 8.8% of the time.
  • They play the move a human actually played from that position, usually the mistake that set up the puzzle, 11.5% of the time. That's more often than they find the best move. Copying a familiar human move is what a pattern-matcher would do.

The catches

  • The 97% had help. When a model gave an illegal move, the program asked again and showed it every allowed move to pick from. Only about 1 first answer in 10 was legal on its own.
  • The engine search is fairly light, but that doesn't change the answer. Lc0 looked 800 positions ahead for each move. Re-checking 1,000 positions with 10× more search kept the models in nearly the same order, and the blunder rate barely moved (42.0% to 42.1%).

T3 Who is better, and why?

"Which side is better? Explain why in your own words."

The model names a side and writes a short explanation. The answer is marked out of 2.

How it's scored

  • 1 mark for the right side. The model has to name the same side as the engine: White, Black or even ("even" means within half a pawn either way).
  • 1 mark for the right idea. Each puzzle on Lichess is labelled with its tactic, such as "fork", "pin" or "checkmate". The computer looks for that word, or one meaning the same, as a whole word in the explanation.
Example

The puzzle's label is "fork", and the engine says White is ahead.

The model writes: "White is better because the knight attacks the king and rook at once, a fork." That gets both marks: 2 out of 2.

An earlier version found words inside other words, so "estimate" counted as "mate" and "opinion" as "pin". That has been fixed.

30%named the better side correctly
54%what you'd get by always naming the side that is usually better
0.76 / 2best model (Gemma 4 26B). Worst: CodeLlama 34B, 0.31

The catches

  • Worse than a fixed guess. The models name the better side less often than someone who always gave the same answer.
  • The puzzle hadn't started yet. The benchmark showed each puzzle's position one move before the tactic appears. So the "right idea" mark is unfair, and it isn't used as evidence. The move and evaluation scores aren't affected.
  • Only checks for words. The computer can't tell whether an explanation makes sense, only whether the right words are there.

Can teaching them help?

If the models fail because they can't "see" the board, teaching them the board should help. Five small models were given extra training, then tested again on exactly the same questions.

  • Experiment 1: where the pieces are. It worked: they went from finding 1–19% of the pieces to 89–99%. But nothing else improved. Their moves became less legal, and their blunders and evaluations didn't get better.
  • Experiment 2: the pieces and every legal move. Before training, I wrote down what would count as success. The first test was whether they actually learned the legal moves, at least 90% right. They didn't: they found only 65–81% of them, and a lot of the moves they listed weren't legal. So this experiment can't say whether knowing the rules would help. Their moves still became less legal, not more.

The conclusion: these models have learned what chess looks like (the notation, the vocabulary, the usual moves), not how it works. Bigger models mostly aren't better: only Google's Gemma models improve with size. Every result is more consistent with pattern matching than with reasoning, though none of it proves that on its own.
