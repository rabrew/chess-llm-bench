# Chess LLM Benchmark — Findings

**Ryan Brew · Project PR09426, *Do LLMs Reason? Benchmarking Logical Performance via Chess***
**Draft of 2026-10-24.** These are the base-benchmark findings after the October 2026 audit, plus the
first fine-tuning result. The legal-moves fine-tune is still running, and its section is marked
pending.

> **How to read the numbers.** Every table below is generated by `scripts/render_findings.py` from
> `results/metrics/` and the fine-tuning results. `python scripts/render_findings.py --check` fails if
> any table no longer matches the data. `summary.json` records the git commit, the data hash and the
> engine settings the numbers came from (`provenance`). Numbers in the running text are quoted from
> these tables. The audit that changed earlier figures is summarised at the end and logged in full in
> `docs/issues-log.md` (Issues 14–23).

**Data.**
- 4,000 Lichess puzzle positions (1,000 per rating tier) × 22 open-source models (2B–70B) × 6 prompt
  formats, giving 526,662 evaluations.
- The six formats are three combined prompts (`fen_only`, `pgn+fen`, `cot`) and three single-task
  prompts (`eval_only`, `move_only`, `explanation_only`). In practice `pgn+fen` is a repeat of
  `fen_only` (Issue 23).

**Ground truth (Lc0, chosen for GPU throughput):**
- **T1 evaluation:** a single forward pass of the Lc0 network, converted to centipawns and capped at
  ±10,000.
- **T2 best move and centipawn loss (CPL):** Lc0 at 800 nodes. Both endpoints are clamped to ±1,000
  (Lichess convention), and both are measured from the same 800-node search (Issue 22).
- **T3 explanation:** a rule-based mark for naming the better side (P1) and the puzzle's theme (P2),
  using whole-word matching.

## Main conclusion (interim — base models)

The models behave like **pattern matchers, not players**. Five lines of evidence point the same way.
Each is "more consistent with" pattern matching; none proves it.

1. **They rarely produce a legal move unaided.** Asked for an evaluation, a move and an explanation
   together, the model's own move is legal only about 1 time in 10. The 97% legality figure is reached
   only after the system re-asks with the full list of legal moves to choose from (Finding 2).
2. **Their moves are barely better than chance.** They pick the engine's best move 10.2% of the time.
   A uniformly random legal move would pick it 8.8% of the time. And they play the *human* move that
   led into the puzzle (usually the mistake that allowed the tactic) **more often** than the engine's
   best move (Finding 1).
3. **Move quality is "perfect or disaster".** About 19% of legal moves are essentially perfect, and
   42% throw away a winning position or worse (Finding 1).
4. **Their evaluations don't follow the position.** No model's evaluation error beats answering "0"
   every time, and the rank correlation with the engine is at most 0.25 (Finding 3).
5. **Size doesn't fix it.** From 2B to 70B the picture is the same, with one exception: both Google
   Gemma families improve with size, while Llama, Qwen and DeepSeek-R1 do not (Finding 5).

Whether teaching the board and the rules changes any of this is the subject of the fine-tuning
experiment (section F). The final conclusion waits for it.

**How to read this table:**
- **Model ranks.** Every model's interval overlaps its neighbour's, so adjacent ranks are
  statistically tied. Only gaps across several places are real, such as Gemma 4 31B/26B (36–37%)
  against the rest (40–45%).
- **"Best move ×random"** compares the model's rate of playing the engine's top move with a random
  legal move on the same positions, which on average have about 27 legal moves to choose from. Most
  models sit between 0.94× and 1.23×. Only Gemma 4 31B and 26B reach about 1.5–1.6×. The old "3–4×
  random" claim used 1 ÷ (average number of legal moves) instead of the average of 1 ÷ (number of
  legal moves), and so set the chance level far too low.
- **"Setup move"** is how often the model plays the move the human actually played from this
  position. That move is the start of the Lichess puzzle (Issue 21) and usually the mistake that
  allowed the tactic. Pooled, models play it 11.5% of the time, more often than they find the
  engine's best move. Reproducing a familiar human move rather than calculating is what pattern
  matching predicts.

## Finding 2: Models rarely produce a legal move without help

On combined prompts the worker accepts a move in three steps:
1. Take the model's `Move:` answer.
2. If it is illegal, look for any legal move elsewhere in the reply.
3. If that fails, ask again with a `move_only` prompt that **lists every legal move** and tells the
   model to pick one.

Final legality is therefore mostly a measure of copying from that list. The base re-run saved the raw
replies, so the model's own first answer can be scored:

<!-- table:first_answer -->
| Model | First answer legal | Final (after re-asking with the list) |
|---|---|---|
| deepseek-r1:7b | 6.8% | 90.9% |
| gemma3:4b | 9.9% | 98.8% |
| llama3.2:3b | 6.9% | 98.4% |
| mistral:7b | 12.6% | 93.7% |
| qwen2.5:7b | 10.3% | 98.4% |
<!-- /table:first_answer -->

The illegal first answers are mostly stock opening moves that ignore the position. llama3.2:3b
answers "e4" 45% of the time, qwen2.5:7b "Nf6" 24%, and gemma3:4b "d5" 20%, often for the wrong side
or onto occupied squares (Issue 20).

Only these five models have first-answer figures. The other 17 were run before raw replies were saved.

## Finding 3: Evaluations do not follow the position

T1 asks for the engine evaluation in centipawns.

- **Rank correlation.** The headline measure is Spearman ρ between the model's number and the engine's,
  which needs no threshold. It is 0.11 pooled, and at most 0.25 for any model (models table).
- **Error vs always answering 0.** No model's average error beats answering 0 cp every time: the ratio
  runs from 1.31 to 7.03. Most positions are near level, so small guesses score a low error without
  any reading. Raw error alone made weak models look good (Issue 14).
- **Direction ("who is winning") accuracy, as a robustness check.** It rises with the threshold
  (44.5% at ±0 cp, 61.6% at ±100, 78.9% at ±200), but chance-corrected agreement (Cohen's κ) stays at
  0.02–0.07 at every threshold. The high raw accuracy at wide thresholds comes from the class
  balance, not from reading the board. The earlier "play vs reason" story, built on direction accuracy
  at ±100, does not survive the chance correction.
- **Perspective check.** No model reports from the side-to-move's point of view: ρ has the same sign
  with White and with Black to move for every model (`t1_by_side_to_move.csv`). Llama-3.3-70B's poor
  direction score is noise, not inversion. (Its `move_only` and retry answers are affected by a
  context-window limit; see Limitations.)

## Finding 4: Explanations name the better side less often than a fixed guess would

- **The side mark (T3 P1):** models name the better side correctly 30.3% of the time. Always naming the
  side that is usually better scores 54.2%, and κ = 0.07 (headline table). Models also rarely say
  "Equal", so they lose the mark on level positions almost automatically.
- **The theme mark:** models name a tactical theme of the puzzle in 6.0% of explanations. But the
  benchmark shows the position *before* the puzzle starts (Issue 21), so the tactic usually isn't on
  the board yet. This mark is reported, but it is not evidence either way.
- **T3 score (0–2):** Gemma 4 26B and 31B lead (0.76, 0.66), as they do on moves. The worst is
  CodeLlama-34B (0.31).

- **Llama 3** (3B → 8B → 70B): the blunder rate goes 41.1% → 44.4% → 43.2%, so no gain from 23×

## Finding 6: Chain-of-thought prompting doesn't help, and answers are not repeatable

<!-- table:formats -->
| Prompt format | T1 ρ | Legal (final) | Blunder rate | T3 (0–2) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| cot | 0.13 | 97.2% | 41.5% | 0.49 |
| eval_only | 0.09 | — | — | — |
| explanation_only | — | — | — | 0.52 |
| fen_only | 0.12 | 97.1% | 41.5% | 0.52 |
| move_only | — | 96.5% | 42.9% | — |
| pgn+fen | 0.11 | 97.1% | 41.5% | 0.51 |
<!-- /table:formats -->

- **Chain of thought:** asking for step-by-step reasoning (`cot`) gives the same blunder rate and
  legality as the plain prompt (`fen_only`).
- **Move history:** `pgn+fen` contained no move history for any position, so it repeats `fen_only`
  (Issue 23). The PGN question remains open.
- **Repeatability:** the repeat is useful in its own right. Given the identical prompt twice, the same
  model gave the same move only 68.7% of the time, named the same better side 75.0% of the time, and
  gave the identical eval 34.2% of the time. Models were sampled at Ollama's default temperature, so
  the **model outputs are not reproducible**, even though the dataset and scoring are.

## Section F: Fine-tuning — does teaching the board change anything?

**Pieces-only fine-tune (done).**
- **What was trained:** five models (llama3.2:3b, gemma3:4b, qwen2.5:7b, mistral:7b, deepseek-r1:7b)
  were trained with LoRA to list every piece and its square from a FEN (20,000 positions, none from
  the benchmark).
- **How they were compared:** with the same benchmark, settings and positions as a re-run of the base
  models on the same code (base → fine-tuned):

|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| deepseek-r1:7b | 1.4% → 90.1% | 0.0% → 3.0% | 0.00 → -0.00 | 90.5% → 69.6% | 41.3% → 42.3% | 0.50 → 0.39 |
| gemma3:4b | 2.2% → 92.6% | 0.0% → 30.0% | 0.03 → 0.05 | 98.7% → 58.0% | 43.0% → 43.3% | 0.39 → 0.35 |
| llama3.2:3b | 11.4% → 89.4% | 0.0% → 9.5% | 0.01 → 0.03 | 98.4% → 71.7% | 41.0% → 41.5% | 0.40 → 0.46 |
| mistral:7b | 18.7% → 99.4% | 0.0% → 13.5% | 0.07 → 0.02 | 93.4% → 90.0% | 39.5% → 42.2% | 0.57 → 0.47 |
| qwen2.5:7b | 5.4% → 96.1% | 0.0% → 36.5% | 0.14 → 0.06 | 98.4% → 95.1% | 42.0% → 44.4% | 0.35 → 0.34 |
<!-- /table:finetune -->

- **Board reading:** it improved enormously, from 1–19% piece recall to 89–99%.
- **Everything else:** nothing improved. T1 ρ stayed near 0, and the blunder rate rose slightly for all
  five models.
- **Legality:** it *fell* for all five, by up to 41 points (gemma3:4b).
- **Why legality fell:** probing the failures showed well-formed but illegal moves (92 of 100), even
  with the list of legal moves in the prompt (Issue 20).
- **Conclusion:** knowing where the pieces are did not help the models choose, judge or even
  legally form moves. That fits pattern matching better than reasoning.
- **Caveat:** the criteria for judging this version were written after its results were known (F-6).

**Pieces + legal moves fine-tune (running, results pending).**
- **What is trained:** the same five models, the same positions and the same settings. The target
  adds every legal move to the piece list.
- **Success criteria:** fixed before any results, in `fine-tuning/specs/legal-moves-finetuning.md`
  (commit `1cf6cd5`). In short:
  - Did the training work? Legal-move recall on held-out positions ≥ 0.9.
  - Does it transfer? First-answer legality up by ≥ 10 points.
  - Is the legality loss undone?
  - Does play or evaluation improve? Blunder rate or ρ.
- **If only legality improves**, that reads as "learned the rules, not chess". This section will be
  filled in when the run finishes.

## Scope and limitations

- **Scope.**
  - The models are open-source, 2B–70B, run locally through Ollama, on chess only.
  - There are no claims about commercial models or about LLM reasoning in general.
  - The positions are Lichess puzzles, so they are tactical by construction.
- **Contamination.** Lichess puzzles are public and may be in the models' training data. That makes the
  failures more striking, not less: the models do badly even on positions they may have seen.
- **Setup position, not the puzzle (Issue 21).** T1, T2 and the side mark are scored on the position
  shown and are valid. The theme mark and the difficulty tiers describe the next position. A corrected
  run would need the puzzle positions themselves (`data/puzzle_meta.json` has them).
- **Reproducibility.** The positions (seed 42), the engine truth and the scoring are reproducible. The
  model outputs are not: default temperature, and 68.7% same-move agreement on repeated prompts.
- **First-answer legality** exists only for the five re-run models, because raw replies were not saved
  earlier.
- **Llama-3.3-70B context (M-1).** Its 512-token context is shorter than 8.7% of `move_only` prompts,
  which Ollama therefore cut off. The same prompt is used for the retry, so the model's `move_only` and
  retried moves are affected. Its other prompts fit.
- **DeepSeek-R1 thinking mode (M-2).**
  - Probably on in the April runs and off in the October re-run.
  - Legality and ρ match across the two to within 0.3 points.
  - The earlier claim that chain-of-thought explained DeepSeek-R1's T1 results is withdrawn.
- **Missing data.** deepseek-r1:7b is missing about 4% of its evaluations in the base run.

## How the audit changed the numbers (W-7)

Every correction below was made in post-processing, without re-running any model except the
documented base re-run. Each has a spec, tests and an entry in `docs/issues-log.md`.

| Claim | Naive figure | Corrected | Why |
|---|---|---|---|
| Legal moves | 64.6% | 97.0% final, **9.3% first answer** | Prompts that never ask for a move were counted as illegal; then the re-ask with the legal-move list was hidden inside the figure (Issues 16, 20) |
| Mean CPL | 4,735 raw | 788 clamped; blunder rate 41.9% | Mate scores, then a mismatched "before" eval (Issue 22) |
| Best move vs chance | 10.2% vs "3.6%" | 10.2% vs **8.8%** | 1 ÷ mean instead of the mean of 1 ÷ n |
| T1 skill | "low error" | worse than always answering 0 | No baseline (Issue 14) |
| Direction accuracy | 46% at ±50 | κ 0.02–0.07 at every threshold | Threshold choice and class balance |
| T3 theme | substring match | whole-word match | "mate" was matched inside "material" (T3-1, Issue 19) |
| Fine-tuned T3 "improved" | 4 of 5 | 1 of 5 | The comparison used the old matcher (Issue 19) |
| Base vs fine-tuned legality | "not comparable" | comparable | All runs had the same rescue; the re-run matches to 0.3 points (Issue 18, corrected) |

The audit is itself a result: naive metrics made weak models look capable at every step.
